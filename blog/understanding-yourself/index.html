Self-worth

Understanding Yourself: Beyond What You Achieve

Exploring self-worth, approval and the parts of you that do not need to earn their place.

MannPriya reflections · Adapted from original writing by Priyansi Kanakia

Who are you when nothing is being measured?

A day can look successful from the outside and still leave you feeling that you have not done enough. You finished the work, answered the messages and showed up for other people. Yet the question returns: was it enough to make you worthwhile? Priyansi’s original reflection invites a different question: who are you outside your achievements?

This is not an argument against ambition. Education, work and caring for family can hold real meaning. But a qualification, promotion or completed task cannot describe everything about a person. You also have preferences, relationships, humour, limits and interests that may never appear on a CV. They remain part of you on an ordinary, unproductive afternoon.

Notice whose approval you are seeking

Sometimes we learn to introduce ourselves through what other people value. Being helpful, capable or easy to get along with can become the safest description available. You might notice yourself agreeing before checking your own preference, or feeling guilty about resting while someone else is busy. Noticing this is a starting point, not another reason to criticise yourself.

Family expectations, academic comparisons and workplace demands can each influence what success means to us. They will not affect everyone in the same way. Rather than assuming one explanation, ask which expectations you genuinely share and which you are carrying because you fear disappointing someone. It is possible to care about people while recognising that their priorities are not always yours.

Kindness can include your own needs

The original piece distinguishes generosity from agreeing out of fear. From the outside they can look similar; on the inside, they may feel different. A freely chosen yes might feel meaningful, even when it takes effort. A reluctant yes may leave resentment or exhaustion. Neither feeling makes you selfish. It gives you information about the choice you are making.

You do not have to begin with a dramatic confrontation. Where it is safe, you could ask for time before answering a request, state what you can realistically offer, or acknowledge that you would prefer something else. Money, housing and responsibilities can limit your options. A boundary should fit your circumstances, not an idealised picture of complete freedom.

Make room for an unscored life

Try describing yourself without mentioning a job, degree, relationship role or accomplishment. What do you enjoy noticing? What matters to you when nobody is watching? Which activities feel worthwhile without being useful? If answers do not come easily, there is no need to force them. This is a reflection to return to, not an assessment you can fail.

You might begin by protecting a little room for something you like: music on the commute, a conversation without multitasking, or a hobby that stays a hobby. The point is not to turn rest into another performance. It is to remember that your life contains more than evidence of productivity. You do not need to complete a better version of yourself before your needs deserve consideration.
