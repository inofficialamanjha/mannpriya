Patterns & compassion

The Person Behind the Patterns

Moving from “What is wrong with me?” towards curiosity, responsibility and a different next step.

MannPriya reflections · Adapted from original writing by Priyansi Kanakia

Describe the pattern, not your character

Perhaps you go quiet when a conversation gets tense, say yes before you have time to think, or replay a small mistake long after the day ends. It is easy to turn these observations into an identity: “I am weak,” or “I always ruin things.” Priyansi’s original reflection asks us to look at the person underneath that judgement.

A more useful description is often smaller and more precise. “I did not say what I needed in that conversation” leaves room to understand what happened. “I can never stand up for myself” can close that room down. The aim is not to put a positive spin on something painful. It is to separate a particular response from the whole of who you are.

Ask what the response may have offered

The original article offers a question: “What did this pattern once protect me from?” You might adapt it to “What am I trying to avoid right now?” Perhaps silence avoids an argument, perfectionism avoids criticism, or immediate agreement avoids an uncomfortable pause. These are possibilities to consider, not explanations that a reader should assume are true for everyone.

You do not need to find a childhood memory to make sense of the present. Some habits may relate to earlier experiences; others may be shaped by current pressures or relationships. A response can be understandable and still have costs. Recognising those costs does not require blaming your family, excusing another person’s behaviour or accepting a story that does not fit you.

Compassion and responsibility can coexist

Being gentler with yourself does not mean that everything you do is harmless. Withdrawing without explanation may leave someone confused. Agreeing and later cancelling may affect other people’s plans. You can acknowledge the impact, apologise where appropriate and still ask what made the moment difficult. Self-punishment is not a prerequisite for taking responsibility.

The same distinction applies to the inner critic. A harsh thought may sound like an attempt to prevent mistakes, but you do not have to accept its language as fact. Try identifying the practical concern underneath it. “I need to prepare for this meeting” gives you something to work with. “I am useless” offers a judgement, not a plan.

Choose one response that fits the situation

Consider a recent, manageable example rather than the most painful experience you can remember. What happened just before your familiar response? What did you need? What did you do next? If writing helps, keep your notes brief and private. The aim is to notice a sequence, not to build a case against yourself or repeatedly revisit something overwhelming.

An alternative might be asking for a moment to think, sending a clarification, or returning to a conversation when you have the words. What is possible depends on safety and context, especially where there are unequal responsibilities or power. You may repeat the old response again. That does not erase your observation. Understanding a pattern is an ongoing enquiry, not a promise that awareness alone will make it disappear.
