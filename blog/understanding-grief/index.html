Grief & loss

Understanding Grief: Room for What You Have Lost

Loss does not follow a neat timetable. A reflection on making room for grief without rushing it.

MannPriya reflections · Adapted from original writing by Priyansi Kanakia

Some losses are harder to name

Grief may follow the death of someone important to you. It can also be part of a relationship ending, a hoped-for future changing, or leaving behind a place that felt like home. These experiences are not identical, and there is no need to compare them. Priyansi’s original reflection makes room for losses that may not be visible to other people.

You might struggle to explain why something still matters when life appears to have moved on. The loss may include routines, a shared language, plans or the person you expected to become. Naming what you miss can be more useful than deciding whether you are entitled to miss it. You do not need someone else’s agreement before acknowledging that something mattered.

There is no required emotional order

Sadness is not the only feeling that may accompany loss. You might notice anger, relief, uncertainty or a stretch of feeling very little. These experiences can sit alongside one another. A lighter afternoon does not make your loss less real; a difficult anniversary does not mean you have gone back to the beginning. There is no performance of grief that you owe anyone.

Expectations around mourning can differ even within one family. Some people want company or familiar rituals; others need quiet or distance from questions. Festivals, family gatherings and everyday reminders may feel different after a loss. You can decide what participation is manageable without making that decision permanent. A choice for this week does not have to become a rule for the future.

Let support be practical as well as emotional

When attention and energy feel limited, ordinary responsibilities may become harder to manage. If it is possible, consider what can wait and what help would actually be useful. Someone bringing a meal, accompanying you to an appointment or helping with paperwork may offer more than an instruction to stay strong. Specific requests can make it easier for willing people to respond.

You do not have to speak about everything to receive support. You might say, “I would like company, but I do not want to discuss it today.” If you notice physical symptoms, avoid assuming that grief explains them. A qualified health professional can assess concerns about your health. A reflection cannot determine the cause of what you are experiencing.

Remembering without a deadline

The original piece suggests small rituals, talking, writing and allowing room to rest. Treat these as options, not instructions. You might keep a photograph nearby, prepare a familiar recipe, write something you do not intend to share, or choose not to mark an occasion. What feels comforting to one person may feel painful to another, and your preference can change.

There is no obligation to find a lesson, gratitude or a stronger version of yourself in loss. Sometimes the task is simply getting through an ordinary day with the support available. If grief feels overwhelming or you want a private place to talk, seeking professional support is an option. You do not have to reach a particular milestone or wait until you are struggling more to ask about it.
